Heredity and Evolution for CBSE Class 10: Mendel's Laws Simplified

Published on November 10, 2026

Heredity and Evolution is one of the most important chapters in the CBSE Class 10 Science curriculum. Understanding Mendel's Laws is crucial not just for your board exams but also for building a strong foundation in biology. This comprehensive guide breaks down complex concepts into easy-to-understand explanations that align perfectly with your NCERT textbook.

Understanding Inheritance: The Foundation of Heredity

NCERT Ch. 9 Heredity refers to the transmission of characteristics from parents to offspring. Every organism inherits traits from its parents through genes, which are segments of DNA that code for specific characteristics. When two parents reproduce, their genetic material combines to create a unique offspring with a mix of traits from both parents.

The concept of heredity forms the basis of understanding why you look like your parents and why siblings can be different from each other despite having the same parents. In CBSE Class 10, you need to understand both the mechanism and the mathematical patterns of inheritance, which is exactly what Gregor Mendel discovered through his groundbreaking experiments with pea plants.

Key characteristics you should know about heredity:

  • Genes are the units of heredity located on chromosomes
  • Alleles are different versions of the same gene
  • Dominant traits mask recessive traits when both are present
  • Inheritance follows predictable mathematical patterns called Mendel's Laws

💡 Common Exam Mistake

Students often confuse genes with alleles. Remember: A gene is a section of DNA coding for a trait, while an allele is a variant form of that gene. For example, the gene for pea plant height has two alleles: T (tall) and t (short).

Mendel's First Law: Law of Segregation

NCERT Ch. 9 The Law of Segregation is Mendel's most fundamental discovery. This law states that each trait is controlled by two factors (now called alleles), and these two factors separate during gamete formation, ensuring that each gamete receives only one allele for each trait.

Mendel discovered this law by conducting experiments with pea plants. When he crossed two pure-breeding plants (homozygous), all offspring in the F1 generation showed only one trait (the dominant one). In the F2 generation, obtained by crossing F1 plants, the recessive trait reappeared in a ratio of 3:1 (three dominant to one recessive).

The 3:1 ratio explained:

  • TT (homozygous dominant) - 25% = tall
  • Tt (heterozygous) - 50% = tall
  • tt (homozygous recessive) - 25% = short

This law is critical for your board exam. You must be able to:

  • Draw Punnett squares for monohybrid crosses
  • Calculate genotypic and phenotypic ratios
  • Distinguish between dominant and recessive alleles
  • Write genetic notation correctly (capital letters for dominant, lowercase for recessive)

Mendel's Second Law: Law of Independent Assortment

NCERT Ch. 9 The Law of Independent Assortment deals with traits controlled by two or more genes. This law states that alleles of different genes segregate independently during gamete formation. In other words, the inheritance of one trait does not affect the inheritance of another trait.

Mendel discovered this law by performing dihybrid crosses—crosses involving two traits simultaneously. When he crossed a plant that was homozygous dominant for both seed color (yellow) and seed shape (round) with one that was homozygous recessive for both (green and wrinkled), the F1 generation was all yellow and round, as expected.

However, when F1 plants were self-fertilized, the F2 generation showed a 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio:

  • 9 parts with both dominant traits (yellow, round)
  • 3 parts with first dominant, second recessive (yellow, wrinkled)
  • 3 parts with first recessive, second dominant (green, round)
  • 1 part with both recessive traits (green, wrinkled)

For your board exam, practice dihybrid crosses extensively. This concept appears frequently in long-answer questions and can earn you 4-5 marks. Always use a 16-box Punnett square for dihybrid crosses.

Evolution and Natural Selection: Connecting Heredity to Evolution

NCERT Ch. 10 While Mendel's Laws explain how traits are inherited, evolution explains how species change over time. Natural selection, proposed by Charles Darwin, is the mechanism of evolution. It works through the principle of "survival of the fittest," where organisms better adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing advantageous traits to their offspring.

The relationship between heredity and evolution is straightforward: genetic variation (created through sexual reproduction and mutation) provides the raw material, and natural selection acts on this variation to drive evolutionary change. Over many generations, small changes accumulate, leading to the formation of new species.

Key points for CBSE Class 10:

  • Evolution is a slow, gradual process (in most cases)
  • Natural selection favors beneficial variations
  • Vestigial organs (like the human appendix) are evidence of evolution
  • Fossil records show gradual changes in species over time
  • Homologous structures (like human arm and bat wing) indicate common ancestry

📝 Board Exam Tip

Questions often ask you to explain how heredity and evolution are related. Always mention that genetic variation through sexual reproduction creates diversity, and natural selection acts on this diversity to favor beneficial traits. This connection is frequently tested in 2-3 mark questions.

Evolution is not just about Darwin's theory. The NCERT curriculum also emphasizes modern understanding, including genetic mutations as a source of variation, and the concept of adaptation. Be prepared to explain how a trait becomes more common in a population over generations through the combined effects of heredity and natural selection.

Practice questions typically ask you to:

  • Define evolution and natural selection
  • Explain evidence for evolution (fossils, homologous structures, DNA)
  • Describe how a specific adaptation evolved
  • Connect Mendel's Laws to modern genetics and evolution

This chapter bridges classical genetics with modern evolutionary biology. In your board exam, approximately 3-5 marks may come from this topic, usually split between short-answer questions (2 marks) and long-answer questions (3-5 marks). Make sure you can not only define these concepts but also explain them with examples relevant to the Indian context, such as peppered moths or Darwin's finches.
