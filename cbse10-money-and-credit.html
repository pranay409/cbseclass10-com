Money and Credit for CBSE Class 10 Economics: Key Concepts Explained

Published on October 29, 2026

Money and Credit is a critical chapter in CBSE Class 10 Economics (Chapter 3 of the NCERT textbook) that explores the monetary system, different forms of money, and the credit system in India. Understanding these concepts is essential for scoring well in your board exams, as this chapter typically carries 4-6 marks in the final examination. This comprehensive guide breaks down all key concepts with exam-relevant explanations and common patterns you'll encounter.

Understanding Money: Functions and Forms

NCERT Chapter 3 - Section 3.1

Money is any item or verifiable record that is generally accepted as payment for goods and services in an economy. Before the advent of modern banking systems, barter was the primary method of exchange, which had significant limitations. Money revolutionized trade by serving as a common medium of exchange.

Functions of Money

Money performs several crucial functions in the economy:

  • Medium of Exchange: Money facilitates the exchange of goods and services without requiring a direct barter arrangement
  • Store of Value: Money can be saved and used for future purchases, preserving purchasing power over time
  • Unit of Account: Money serves as a standard measure for pricing goods, services, and debts
  • Standard of Deferred Payment: Money enables loans and credit transactions by establishing a standard for future obligations

Forms of Money in India

India's monetary system comprises different forms of money:

  • Currency Notes and Coins: Physical money issued by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), representing fiat money
  • Demand Deposits: Money held in bank accounts that can be withdrawn on demand
  • Electronic Money: Digital transactions through credit/debit cards, UPI, and online banking
💡 Board Exam Tip: Common questions ask about the difference between money and currency, or why barter was inefficient. Remember: currency is a subset of money. Currency is government-issued, while money includes bank deposits and other liquid assets. For board exams, focus on explaining how money eliminates the problem of "double coincidence of wants" in barter systems.

The Banking System and Credit

NCERT Chapter 3 - Sections 3.2 & 3.3

Banks play a pivotal role in the credit system by accepting deposits and lending money to borrowers. The credit system connects savers (depositors) with borrowers, channeling funds throughout the economy. Understanding how banks operate is fundamental to grasping the money and credit framework in India.

How Banks Create Credit

When you deposit money in a bank, the bank uses a portion of those deposits to provide loans to borrowers. This process of lending deposits is called credit creation. For example, if a bank receives deposits of ₹1,00,000 and maintains a reserve requirement of 10%, it can lend out ₹90,000. This mechanism multiplies the money supply in the economy, as both the depositor and the borrower hold claims to money.

RBI's Role in Money Supply

The Reserve Bank of India controls the money supply through:

  • Open Market Operations (OMO): Buying and selling government securities to adjust liquidity
  • Repo Rate Management: Adjusting the rate at which banks borrow from the RBI
  • Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): Mandating that banks maintain certain reserves
  • Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): Requiring banks to hold specified securities

Credit in the Indian Economy

NCERT Chapter 3 - Section 3.4

Credit refers to the system where a lender provides money or goods to a borrower with the promise of future repayment, often with interest. Credit is essential for economic growth because it enables consumption and investment even when people lack immediate funds. However, credit also carries risks if borrowers default on loans.

Formal and Informal Credit Systems

India has two credit systems operating simultaneously:

  • Formal Credit: Provided by banks, cooperative societies, and government institutions. These lenders follow regulations, charge regulated interest rates, and maintain records. Formal credit is generally more reliable and secure.
  • Informal Credit: Provided by moneylenders, shopkeepers, employers, and relatives. Informal credit often lacks regulation, features higher interest rates, and has less documentation. Many rural households still depend on informal credit.

Terms of Credit

When availing credit, borrowers encounter several terms:

  • Interest Rate: The cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage of the loan amount per annum
  • Collateral: An asset pledged as security for the loan. If the borrower defaults, the lender can claim the collateral
  • Loan Period: The duration within which the borrowed amount must be repaid
  • Mode of Repayment: Whether payments are made in lump sum or installments
⚠️ Common Mistake Alert: Students often confuse formal and informal credit by assuming all banks are formal. While most banks are formal, cooperative credit societies are also formal credit institutions regulated by the government. Additionally, don't assume informal credit is always negative—it serves crucial roles in rural economies, but typically charges higher rates due to higher risk and operational costs.

Challenges and Policy Implications

NCERT Chapter 3 - Section 3.5

India's credit system faces several challenges that board exam questions frequently address:

  • Financial Inclusion: A significant portion of India's population, especially in rural areas, lacks access to formal credit. Government schemes like Jan Dhan Yojana and Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana aim to bridge this gap.
  • Default Risk: Borrowers may fail to repay loans, causing financial stress to lenders. The non-performing assets (NPAs) problem in Indian banks is a critical issue.
  • Interest Rate Disparities: The gap between formal and informal credit interest rates remains substantial, pushing poor households toward exploitative moneylenders.
  • Documentation and Collateral Issues: Many poor households lack collateral or proper documentation, limiting their access to formal credit.

The government has implemented various policies to strengthen the credit system, including Self-Help Groups (SHGs), Microfinance Institutions (MFIs), and targeted lending programs for priority sectors like agriculture.

Exam Pattern Insights

Based on recent CBSE Class 10 board exams, this chapter typically includes:

  • 2-3 Objective Questions (1 mark each): Usually about money's functions or forms of credit
  • 1-2 Short Answer Questions (3 marks each): Explaining terms like collateral, formal credit, or monetary policy tools
  • 1 Long Answer Question (5-6 marks): Comparing formal and informal credit, or discussing credit policies in India
