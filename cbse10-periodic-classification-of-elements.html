Periodic Classification of Elements for CBSE Class 10: Trends Explained

Published on November 06, 2026

The periodic classification of elements is one of the most fundamental concepts in chemistry for CBSE Class 10 students. Understanding how elements are organized and the trends that govern their properties is essential for scoring well in your board exams. This comprehensive guide covers all the key concepts from the NCERT textbook with a focus on exam patterns and common questions.

Introduction to Periodic Classification

The periodic table is a systematic arrangement of all known elements based on their atomic number and chemical properties. In CBSE Class 10, you'll learn about the modern periodic table, which is based on Mendeleev's contributions and the contemporary understanding of atomic structure.

Reference: NCERT Science Class 10, Chapter 5 - Periodic Classification of Elements

The modern periodic table arranges elements in 18 vertical groups (columns) and 7 horizontal periods (rows). Each position in the table tells us about the element's atomic structure, particularly the number of valence electrons in its outermost shell. This organization helps predict an element's chemical properties and reactivity patterns.

Key Periodic Trends Explained

Atomic Radius Trend

Atomic radius refers to the distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron. As you move down a group (from top to bottom), the atomic radius increases because new electron shells are added. Conversely, as you move across a period (from left to right), the atomic radius decreases because electrons are added to the same shell while the nuclear charge increases.

  • Down a group: Atomic radius increases (more electron shells)
  • Across a period: Atomic radius decreases (greater nuclear attraction)

Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom. Elements with high ionization energies are reluctant to lose electrons and tend to be non-metals, while elements with low ionization energies readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations).

  • Down a group: Ionization energy decreases (electrons are farther from nucleus)
  • Across a period: Ionization energy generally increases (stronger nuclear attraction)

Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the tendency of an element to attract electrons toward itself in a chemical bond. Fluorine is the most electronegative element, while alkali metals have the lowest electronegativity. This trend is crucial for understanding covalent bonding and polar molecules.

⚠️ Common Exam Mistake: Students often confuse electronegativity with ionization energy. Remember: electronegativity refers to attraction in a chemical bond, while ionization energy is about removing an electron from an isolated atom. These are related but different concepts.

Understanding Groups and Periods

Reference: NCERT Science Class 10, Chapter 5 - Periodic Table Organization

The periodic table is organized into specific groups that share similar chemical properties:

  • Group 1 (Alkali Metals): Highly reactive metals that form +1 ions. Examples: Lithium (Li), Sodium (Na), Potassium (K)
  • Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals): Reactive metals that form +2 ions. Examples: Magnesium (Mg), Calcium (Ca)
  • Group 17 (Halogens): Highly reactive non-metals that form -1 ions. Examples: Fluorine (F), Chlorine (Cl), Bromine (Br)
  • Group 18 (Noble Gases): Unreactive gases with complete valence shells. Examples: Helium (He), Neon (Ne), Argon (Ar)

Period Trends

As we move across a period from left to right, elements transition from metals to metalloids to non-metals. The atomic number increases, and the number of valence electrons increases from 1 to 8 (with slight variations). This explains why elements in the same period have different properties despite being in the same "row" of the periodic table.

Valency and Chemical Reactivity

Valency is the combining capacity of an element, determined by the number of valence electrons (electrons in the outermost shell). Elements aim to complete their valence shell by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

  • Metals: Tend to lose valence electrons (positive valency)
  • Non-metals: Tend to gain valence electrons (negative valency)
  • Noble gases: Have complete valence shells (valency = 0)

The periodic table's organization makes predicting valency straightforward: the group number often indicates the valency for representative elements. For example, Group 1 elements have a valency of +1, Group 17 elements have a valency of -1, and Group 18 elements typically have a valency of 0.

✓ Board Exam Tip: Questions on periodic trends and valency are extremely common in CBSE Class 10 board exams. Practice drawing the periodic table and memorizing the first 30 elements with their atomic numbers, symbols, and positions. This foundation will help you answer most periodic classification questions correctly.

Application to Exam Patterns

CBSE Class 10 exams typically ask questions about:

  1. Identifying elements based on periodic table position and properties
  2. Explaining periodic trends (atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity)
  3. Predicting chemical reactivity and valency
  4. Understanding the basis for Mendeleev's classification and its improvements
  5. Short answer questions on specific element groups and their characteristics

Practice questions often include scenarios where you need to compare two elements and predict their relative reactivity or properties based on their periodic table positions. Understanding the "why" behind each trend will help you tackle these questions confidently.
