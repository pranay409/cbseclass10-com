Life Processes for CBSE Class 10: Nutrition, Respiration and Transport

Published on November 07, 2026

Life processes are the fundamental biological activities that sustain life in all living organisms. According to the NCERT Class 10 Science curriculum (Chapter 6), understanding these processes is crucial for board exams. The three primary life processes—nutrition, respiration, and transport—are interconnected systems that work together to maintain homeostasis and enable survival.

In this comprehensive guide, we'll explore each process in detail, focusing on what CBSE Class 10 students need to know for their board examinations. These topics frequently appear as 3-mark and 5-mark questions in the Science paper, making them essential for scoring well.

1. Nutrition: The Foundation of Life Processes

Nutrition is the process by which organisms obtain and utilize food to meet their energy and growth requirements. The NCERT textbook divides nutrition into two main types: autotrophic nutrition and heterotrophic nutrition.

Autotrophic Nutrition: This is primarily found in plants and involves the production of organic compounds from inorganic substances using light energy (photosynthesis) or chemical energy (chemosynthesis). The equation for photosynthesis is:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Students must understand that photosynthesis occurs in two stages: the light-dependent reactions in the thylakoid membrane and the light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) in the stroma of chloroplasts.

Heterotrophic Nutrition: This occurs in animals and can be classified into three types:

  • Holozoic Nutrition: Ingestion of solid food particles followed by digestion, absorption, and egestion (seen in most animals)
  • Saprophytic Nutrition: Organisms like fungi and bacteria feed on dead organic matter
  • Parasitic Nutrition: Parasites obtain nutrients from living host organisms

In humans, digestion involves mechanical and chemical processes. The human digestive system breaks down food into simpler molecules like glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids, which are absorbed in the small intestine and transported throughout the body.

2. Respiration: Energy Generation in Cells

Respiration is the process by which cells release energy from organic compounds. This is one of the most frequently tested topics in CBSE Class 10 Science exams. Respiration is divided into two main types: aerobic respiration and anaerobic respiration.

Aerobic Respiration: This occurs in the presence of oxygen and is highly efficient. The complete oxidation of glucose yields approximately 38 ATP molecules. The process involves three main stages:

  • Glycolysis: Occurs in the cytoplasm; glucose (6C) is converted to pyruvate (3C), producing 2 ATP molecules
  • Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle): Occurs in the mitochondrial matrix; pyruvate is completely oxidized, releasing CO₂ and generating electron carriers
  • Electron Transport Chain: Occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane; electron carriers (NADH and FADH₂) transfer electrons, generating the majority of ATP through oxidative phosphorylation

Anaerobic Respiration: This occurs in the absence of oxygen. In humans, it produces lactate (lactic acid), causing muscle fatigue. In plants and microorganisms, it produces ethanol and CO₂ (fermentation). Anaerobic respiration yields only 2 ATP molecules per glucose, making it much less efficient than aerobic respiration.

The respiratory quotient (RQ) is an important concept: RQ = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed. For carbohydrates, RQ = 1; for fats, RQ = 0.7; for proteins, RQ = 0.9.

3. Transport: Movement of Substances in Organisms

Transport refers to the movement of materials across cell membranes and within organisms. In NCERT Class 10, transport is categorized into passive transport and active transport.

Passive Transport: This does not require energy and includes:

  • Diffusion: Movement of molecules from areas of higher concentration to lower concentration
  • Osmosis: Movement of water molecules across a semipermeable membrane

Active Transport: This requires ATP energy and allows cells to move substances against the concentration gradient. Examples include the sodium-potassium pump and nutrient uptake in plant roots.

In plants, xylem transports water and minerals upward through the root pressure and transpiration pull mechanism. Phloem transports sugars and organic compounds through turgor pressure differences (mass flow hypothesis). In animals, the circulatory system transports oxygen, nutrients, and hormones, while the lymphatic system drains excess tissue fluid and supports immunity.

⚠️ Common Exam Mistakes

Students often confuse aerobic and anaerobic respiration or forget that photosynthesis is the reverse of respiration. Remember: Photosynthesis stores energy in glucose, while respiration releases this energy. Also, don't mix up xylem (water transport) with phloem (nutrient transport) transport directions!

4. Integration of Life Processes

These three life processes work together seamlessly. Nutrition provides the raw materials and energy substrates. Respiration breaks down these materials to release energy in the form of ATP. Transport distributes these resources and ATP throughout the organism, enabling all other life activities.

For effective learning, create concept maps showing how photosynthesis in plants produces glucose, which is used in respiration to generate ATP, which powers active transport and other cellular functions. Understanding these relationships will help you answer complex board exam questions that integrate multiple topics.

Practice previous years' CBSE Class 10 Science board papers to familiarize yourself with the question patterns. Typically, you'll encounter 1-mark factual questions, 3-mark comparative questions, and 5-mark detailed explanation questions on these topics.
