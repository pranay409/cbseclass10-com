Carbon and Its Compounds for CBSE Class 10: Bonding and Nomenclature

Published on November 05, 2026

Carbon and its compounds form one of the most important chapters in CBSE Class 10 Science (Chemistry). This chapter, typically found in NCERT Chapter 4, introduces students to the fundamental properties of carbon, its bonding patterns, and the classification of organic compounds. Understanding carbon chemistry is crucial not only for board exams but also for competitive exams like JEE and NEET in the future.

Understanding Carbon's Unique Properties

Carbon is the fourth most abundant element in the universe and the second most abundant element in the human body (after oxygen by mass). What makes carbon unique is its tetravalency – it has four valence electrons and can form four covalent bonds. This property allows carbon to create an enormous variety of compounds.

Carbon atoms can bond with:

  • Other carbon atoms (forming long chains and rings)
  • Hydrogen atoms (forming hydrocarbons)
  • Oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and other non-metallic elements

The ability of carbon to form chains of varying lengths and arrangements leads to the formation of millions of organic compounds – far more than all inorganic compounds combined. This self-linking property of carbon atoms is called catenation.

Covalent Bonding: The Foundation of Carbon Chemistry

According to NCERT Chapter 4, carbon primarily forms covalent bonds with other atoms. A covalent bond is formed by the sharing of electron pairs between atoms. This is different from ionic bonding (seen in inorganic compounds) where electrons are transferred from one atom to another.

Types of Covalent Bonds in Carbon Compounds

Single Covalent Bond (σ bond): A single bond involves the sharing of one pair of electrons. In ethane (C₂H₆), carbon atoms are connected by a single bond: H₃C–CH₃. Single bonds are relatively strong and allow rotation around the bond axis.

Double Covalent Bond (σ + π bond): A double bond consists of one sigma (σ) bond and one pi (π) bond, totaling two shared electron pairs. Ethene (C₂H₄) contains a carbon-carbon double bond: H₂C=CH₂. Double bonds restrict rotation and are shorter and stronger than single bonds.

Triple Covalent Bond (σ + 2π bonds): A triple bond contains one sigma and two pi bonds. Ethyne (C₂H₂) has a triple bond: HC≡CH. Triple bonds are the strongest and shortest carbon-carbon bonds, and they prevent rotation entirely.

⚡ Key Exam Tip

Remember: Single bonds allow free rotation, double bonds restrict rotation, and triple bonds prevent rotation entirely. This property affects the geometric structures and reactivity of molecules – a concept frequently tested in CBSE board exams.

Nomenclature of Carbon Compounds (NCERT IUPAC Rules)

The NCERT textbook teaches students the IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) nomenclature system for naming organic compounds. This systematic approach is essential for scoring well in the nomenclature section of board exams.

Rules for Naming Hydrocarbons

Step 1: Identify the parent chain – Select the longest chain of carbon atoms. This determines the root name of the compound.

Step 2: Apply IUPAC prefixes based on the number of carbons:

  • 1 carbon: Meth-
  • 2 carbons: Eth-
  • 3 carbons: Prop-
  • 4 carbons: But-
  • 5 carbons: Pent-
  • 6 carbons: Hex-

Step 3: Identify the functional group – Add a suffix based on the type of bond:

  • -ane for single bonds (alkanes)
  • -ene for double bonds (alkenes)
  • -yne for triple bonds (alkynes)

Step 4: Number the chain – Start from the end that gives the multiple bond or functional group the lowest number.

Example: CH₃-CH=CH-CH₃ is named but-2-ene (not but-3-ene) because the double bond is closer to the second carbon when numbered from the left.

Alkanes, Alkenes, and Functional Groups

The CBSE Class 10 exam focuses heavily on three main categories of organic compounds:

Alkanes: Saturated hydrocarbons containing only single C-C bonds. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Examples include methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), and propane (C₃H₈). Alkanes are relatively unreactive at room temperature.

Alkenes: Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing one or more C=C double bonds. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ. Examples include ethene (C₂H₄) and propene (C₃H₆). Alkenes are more reactive than alkanes due to the presence of the π bond.

Alkynes: Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing one or more C≡C triple bonds. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Examples include ethyne (C₂H₂). Alkynes are even more reactive than alkenes.

Functional Groups: Specific groups of atoms within molecules that determine the chemical properties and reactivity of organic compounds. Common functional groups tested in CBSE include:

  • Alcohol (-OH): Found in compounds like ethanol (C₂H₅OH)
  • Aldehyde (-CHO): Found in formaldehyde (HCHO)
  • Ketone (C=O): Found in acetone
  • Carboxylic acid (-COOH): Found in acetic acid (CH₃COOH)
