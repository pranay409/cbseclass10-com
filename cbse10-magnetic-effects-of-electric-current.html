Magnetic Effects of Electric Current for CBSE Class 10: Complete Notes

Published on November 14, 2026

The concept of magnetic effects of electric current is a fascinating topic in CBSE Class 10 Science that bridges electricity and magnetism. As per the NCERT curriculum, Chapter 13 focuses on this critical subject matter, which has significant weightage in board examinations. Understanding how electric current produces magnetic fields and vice versa is essential for mastering this chapter and securing excellent marks in your Science paper.

1. Magnetic Field Due to Electric Current (NCERT Chapter 13.1-13.2)

When an electric current flows through a conductor, it generates a magnetic field around it. This phenomenon is the foundation of electromagnetism. The strength and direction of the magnetic field depend on the magnitude of the current and the shape of the conductor.

Key Concepts:

  • Magnetic Field Around a Straight Conductor: When current flows through a straight wire, concentric circles of magnetic field are produced around it. The direction can be determined using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule — if your thumb points in the direction of current, your fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field.
  • Magnetic Field in a Solenoid: A solenoid is a coil of insulated copper wire wrapped tightly in a helical pattern. When current passes through it, a uniform magnetic field is produced inside, similar to a bar magnet. The field strength increases with the number of turns and the current flowing through it.
  • Electromagnets: By placing an iron core inside a solenoid, we can create a powerful electromagnet. These are widely used in devices like electric bells, relays, and cranes.

For CBSE board exams, students should practice drawing magnetic field patterns around these conductors and be able to explain the right-hand rule clearly in both short-answer and long-answer questions.

2. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor in a Magnetic Field (NCERT Chapter 13.3)

When a current-carrying conductor is placed in an external magnetic field, it experiences a mechanical force. This principle is crucial for understanding electric motors and other practical applications.

Important Points:

  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule: This rule helps determine the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor. Thumb points in the direction of force, index finger points in the direction of magnetic field, and middle finger points in the direction of current.
  • Magnitude of Force: The force is given by F = BIL, where B is the magnetic field strength, I is the current, and L is the length of the conductor in the field.
  • Applications: Electric motors, galvanometers, and moving-coil loudspeakers all rely on this principle.

Students often confuse Fleming's Left-Hand Rule with the Right-Hand Rule. Remember: Left-Hand for Force (motor effect), Right-Hand for Field/Induction.

3. Electric Motor and Electromagnetic Induction (NCERT Chapter 13.4-13.5)

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy using the principle of force on a current-carrying conductor. This is one of the most important applications tested in CBSE board exams.

Working of an Electric Motor:

  • A rectangular coil (armature) carrying current is placed in a magnetic field between permanent magnets or electromagnets.
  • The force on the two vertical sides of the coil is in opposite directions, creating a torque that rotates the coil.
  • A split-ring commutator ensures that the direction of current reverses every half rotation, maintaining continuous rotation.
  • Brushes (usually made of carbon) maintain electrical contact with the commutator as the coil rotates.

Electromagnetic Induction: This is the reverse process — when a conductor moves through a magnetic field (or the field changes), an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in the conductor. This principle is used in generators and transformers. Faraday's laws of electromagnetic induction describe this phenomenon mathematically.

⚠️ Common Mistake Alert

Many students confuse the working of motors and generators. Remember: Motors convert electrical energy to mechanical energy (use Fleming's Left-Hand Rule), while Generators convert mechanical energy to electrical energy (use Fleming's Right-Hand Rule). This distinction is crucial for scoring well in MCQ and short-answer questions.

4. Exam Patterns & Practice Tips (Board Exam Strategy)

Based on previous CBSE board papers, magnetic effects of electric current typically appears in the following formats:

Question Types:

  • Multiple Choice Questions (1 mark): Usually test your understanding of the Right-Hand Rule, Fleming's Rules, and basic definitions.
  • Short Answer Questions (2-3 marks): Require diagrams with explanations. Common topics include magnetic field patterns, working of electromagnets, and applications of motors.
  • Long Answer Questions (5 marks): Expect detailed explanations of motor construction, Faraday's laws, or electromagnetic induction with numerical problems.

Preparation Strategy:

  • Practice drawing accurate diagrams of solenoids, straight conductors, and electric motor cross-sections.
  • Master both Fleming's rules by consistent practice — use mnemonics or create your own memory tricks.
  • Solve previous years' board papers to understand the exact level of expected answers.
  • Create concept maps linking electricity, magnetism, and their practical applications.
  • Perform simple experiments at home (like the compass needle demonstration around a current-carrying wire) to strengthen conceptual understanding.

This chapter carries approximately 8-10 marks in the CBSE Class 10 Science paper (out of 80 marks), making it a significant contributor to your total score. With focused preparation and consistent practice, you can master this topic completely.
