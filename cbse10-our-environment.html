Our Environment for CBSE Class 10: Ecosystem and Food Chains Explained

Published on November 16, 2026

The chapter "Our Environment" in CBSE Class 10 Science (Chapter 15 of NCERT) is a critical topic for board examinations that covers fundamental ecological concepts. Understanding ecosystems, food chains, and the flow of energy through organisms is essential not only for scoring well in exams but also for developing environmental awareness. This comprehensive guide will help you master the concepts with clarity and confidence.

What is an Ecosystem? Understanding the Basics

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms (biotic components) interact with their physical environment (abiotic components) in a defined area. The NCERT Chapter 15 emphasizes that ecosystems are self-sustaining systems where energy flows and nutrients cycle continuously.

Every ecosystem has two major components:

  • Biotic Components: All living organisms—plants, animals, and microorganisms (producers, consumers, decomposers)
  • Abiotic Components: Physical factors like sunlight, temperature, water, soil, air, and atmospheric gases

Common examples of ecosystems include forests, ponds, grasslands, and even your school garden. Each ecosystem has its unique characteristics and species composition, which CBSE exams frequently ask about.

⚠️ Common Exam Mistake

Students often confuse "habitat" with "ecosystem." A habitat is where an organism lives, while an ecosystem is a community of organisms and their environment interacting together. This distinction frequently appears in CBSE board questions.

Food Chains and Food Webs: Energy Transfer Explained

One of the most heavily examined topics in Chapter 15 is food chains and food webs. The NCERT text defines a food chain as a linear sequence showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one organism to another through feeding relationships.

Structure of a Food Chain

A typical food chain follows this pattern:

  • Producer (Trophic Level I): Green plants that manufacture their own food through photosynthesis (e.g., grass, wheat, algae)
  • Primary Consumer (Trophic Level II): Herbivores that feed on producers (e.g., rabbit, cow, grasshopper)
  • Secondary Consumer (Trophic Level III): Carnivores that feed on primary consumers (e.g., fox, snake, hawk)
  • Tertiary Consumer (Trophic Level IV): Top carnivores that feed on secondary consumers (e.g., lion, eagle)
  • Decomposers: Bacteria and fungi that break down dead organisms and return nutrients to soil

Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle

Food Web vs. Food Chain

While a food chain is linear, a food web shows interconnected food chains in an ecosystem. This is crucial for CBSE exams because food webs better represent real ecological relationships. For instance, a frog may eat multiple insect species, and snakes may feed on various prey items. Understanding this distinction helps you grasp ecosystem complexity and stability.

Energy Flow in Ecosystems (10% Law)

The NCERT emphasizes the concept of energy flow, which is fundamental to understanding how ecosystems function. Energy enters an ecosystem through sunlight, which producers (plants) convert into chemical energy via photosynthesis.

The 10% Law states that only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next. The remaining 90% is lost as heat, used in respiration, or remains locked in dead organisms. This explains why:

  • Ecosystems can support fewer top carnivores than herbivores
  • Energy decreases as we move up the food chain
  • Biomass pyramids are always pyramid-shaped with producers at the base

For CBSE exam questions, remember that if grass contains 10,000 J of energy, a rabbit (primary consumer) will have ~1,000 J, a fox (secondary consumer) ~100 J, and so forth. This calculation-based question frequently appears in board exams.

Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers: Roles in Ecosystems

Every organism plays a specific role in the ecosystem, as detailed in NCERT Chapter 15. Understanding these roles is essential for answering descriptive board exam questions.

Producers (Autotrophs)

Green plants and algae are producers. They synthesize organic compounds from inorganic substances using sunlight. They form the base of all food chains and webs. Without producers, no ecosystem can exist because they are the primary source of energy for all other organisms.

Consumers (Heterotrophs)

Consumers are organisms that feed on other organisms. They are classified as:

  • Herbivores: Feed only on plants (e.g., cow, deer, insect larvae)
  • Carnivores: Feed only on animals (e.g., lion, eagle, snake)
  • Omnivores: Feed on both plants and animals (e.g., bear, human, crow)
  • Scavengers: Feed on dead animals (e.g., vulture, hyena)

Decomposers

Bacteria and fungi are decomposers that break down dead organic matter and release nutrients back into the soil. This recycling is crucial for ecosystem sustainability. CBSE questions often ask why decomposers are important—the answer is that they complete nutrient cycles and prevent accumulation of dead matter.
