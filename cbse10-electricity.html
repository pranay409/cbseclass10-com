Electricity for CBSE Class 10: Ohm's Law and Circuit Numericals

Published on November 13, 2026 | Complete Board Exam Study Guide

Electricity is one of the most critical chapters in CBSE Class 10 Science, forming the foundation for higher physics studies. Chapter 12 of the NCERT textbook covers electric current, circuits, and the fundamental laws governing electrical flow. For board exam success, mastering Ohm's Law and related circuit numericals is absolutely essential. This comprehensive guide will help you understand the concepts deeply and solve complex problems with confidence.

Understanding Electric Current and Ohm's Law (NCERT Chapter 12)

Electric current is the flow of charge through a conductor. Measured in Amperes (A), it represents the amount of charge flowing past a point per unit time. The mathematical definition is I = Q/t, where Q is charge in Coulombs and t is time in seconds.

Ohm's Law is the cornerstone of circuit analysis and states that the electric current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature remains constant. The mathematical form is:

V = IR

Where:

  • V = Potential Difference (in Volts)
  • I = Electric Current (in Amperes)
  • R = Resistance (in Ohms, Ω)

This relationship is linear for ohmic conductors (like metals at constant temperature) and forms the basis for solving nearly all circuit problems in board exams. Understanding the inverse relationship between current and resistance is crucial: as resistance increases, current decreases for a fixed voltage.

Common Misconceptions About Ohm's Law

Many students mistakenly believe Ohm's Law applies to all materials universally. In reality, it only applies to ohmic conductors. Non-ohmic materials like semiconductors and gases show non-linear V-I relationships. The exam frequently tests this distinction.

Resistance and Resistivity (NCERT Section 12.2)

Resistance is the opposition offered by a material to the flow of electric current. It depends on the material's properties and physical dimensions. The resistivity formula is fundamental for CBSE Class 10 boards:

R = ρL/A

Where:

  • ρ (rho) = Resistivity of the material (Ω·m)
  • L = Length of the conductor (m)
  • A = Cross-sectional area (m²)

This formula shows that resistance increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area. A common board exam question asks students to compare resistances of different wire configurations.

⚠️ Key Exam Tip: Always convert units to SI before solving numericals. If length is in cm, convert to m. If area is in mm², convert to m². Unit conversion mistakes account for nearly 30% of numerical errors in board exams.

Factors Affecting Resistance

  • Material of conductor (copper, nichrome, constantan have different resistivities)
  • Temperature (resistance increases with temperature for most metals)
  • Length of the conductor
  • Cross-sectional area of the conductor

Series and Parallel Circuits (NCERT Section 12.3-12.4)

CBSE board exams frequently test the combination of resistances in series and parallel arrangements. Understanding these configurations is essential for solving complex circuit problems.

Series Circuits

In a series circuit, resistances are connected end-to-end. Current flows through each resistor, and the same amount of current passes through all components.

Rules for Series Circuits:

  • Total Resistance: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ... (resistances simply add)
  • Current is constant throughout: I = same at all points
  • Voltage divides: Vtotal = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + ...
  • Power dissipation: More power in larger resistances

Parallel Circuits

In a parallel circuit, resistances are connected between common points. Voltage across each resistor is the same, but current divides among branches.

Rules for Parallel Circuits:

  • Total Resistance: 1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ... (reciprocals add)
  • Voltage is constant: V = same across all branches
  • Current divides: Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + ...
  • Power dissipation: Less power in larger resistances

Solved Numericals and Board Exam Patterns

CBSE Class 10 board exams typically include 2-3 mark questions on circuit analysis. Here's what exam patterns reveal:

Typical Numerical Type 1: Ohm's Law Application

Problem: A wire has a resistance of 10 Ω. When a potential difference of 50 V is applied across it, find the current flowing through the wire.

Solution: Using V = IR, I = V/R = 50/10 = 5 A

Typical Numerical Type 2: Resistance Calculation

Problem: A nichrome wire of length 100 cm and cross-sectional area 0.5 mm² has a resistivity of 100 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Calculate its resistance.

Solution: Converting: L = 1 m, A = 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ m²

R = ρL/A = (100 × 10⁻⁸ × 1)/(0.5 × 10⁻⁶) = 2 Ω

Typical Numerical Type 3: Series/Parallel Combinations

Problem: Three resistances of 5 Ω, 10 Ω, and 15 Ω are connected in series. A potential difference of 60 V is applied. Find: (a) Total resistance (b) Total current (c) Voltage across the 10 Ω resistor.

Solution: (a) Rtotal = 5 + 10 + 15 = 30 Ω

(b) I = V/R = 60/30 = 2 A

(c) V₁₀Ω = IR = 2 × 10 = 20 V

💡 Pro Tip for Board Exams: Always draw circuit diagrams clearly, label components, and show unit conversions. Examiners award partial credit for method even if final answer has calculation errors. Work through 15-20 numericals before the exam—repetition builds speed and accuracy crucial for managing time during 3-hour board exams.

Power and Energy in Electrical Circuits (NCERT Section 12.5)

Power is the rate of energy consumption in a circuit. The fundamental formula is P = VI, with related forms P = I²R and P = V²/R. Energy consumed is E = Pt, measured in Joules
