Power Sharing in Democracies: Belgium and Sri Lanka Case Studies

October 23, 2026

Power sharing is one of the most crucial concepts in the CBSE Class 10 Civics curriculum. It forms the foundation of democratic governance and is essential for exam success. The NCERT Chapter 1: Power Sharing explores how power is distributed among different levels and organs of government to ensure stability, prevent tyranny, and protect the rights of all citizens. Through the real-world examples of Belgium and Sri Lanka, students gain practical insights into how power sharing works—and what happens when it fails.

Understanding Power Sharing: Definition and Importance

Power sharing refers to the distribution of power among various political institutions and social groups in a democracy. It's not just about dividing responsibilities; it's about ensuring that no single person or group holds absolute power, which could lead to oppression and injustice.

In the CBSE Class 10 Civics syllabus, you'll learn that power sharing operates at multiple levels:

  • Horizontal distribution: Power is shared among the three organs of government—Executive, Legislative, and Judiciary (separation of powers).
  • Vertical distribution: Power is shared between central, state, and local governments (federalism).
  • Community-based sharing: Power is shared among different social, religious, and ethnic communities through mechanisms like reserved seats, minority protections, and coalition governments.

The importance of power sharing cannot be overstated. It prevents concentration of authority, ensures democratic participation, protects minority rights, and creates a system of checks and balances. Both CBSE board exams and competitive entrance exams frequently test students' understanding of these concepts, making it essential to master them thoroughly.

Belgium: A Success Story of Power Sharing (NCERT Case Study)

Belgium serves as one of the best examples of successful power sharing in the world and is extensively covered in the CBSE Class 10 Civics curriculum.

The Belgian Context

Belgium is a small European nation with a complex demographic composition: 59% Dutch-speaking Flemish population in the north and 40% French-speaking Walloons in the south. Additionally, the capital Brussels has a mixed population with both communities. Historically, this diversity could have led to conflict, but Belgium's constitutional framework ensures peaceful coexistence.

Belgium's Power Sharing Mechanisms

Belgium has adopted multiple innovative strategies to share power among its communities:

  • Federal Structure: The country is divided into regions (Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels) with significant autonomy over cultural, educational, and economic matters. This vertical distribution of power ensures that each community has control over issues directly affecting them.
  • Community Governments: Separate governments for Dutch-speaking and French-speaking communities handle matters like education, culture, and social welfare. This is crucial for protecting minority interests.
  • Coalition Cabinets: The national government always includes representatives from both major communities. No cabinet can be formed without consensus between Flemish and Walloon leaders.
  • Proportional Representation: The electoral system ensures that political parties receive seats proportional to the votes they receive, preventing any single group from dominating the legislature.
  • Constitutional Safeguards: Special constitutional provisions protect the rights of both communities and require approval from both communities for major legislative changes.

Why Belgium Succeeded

Belgium's power-sharing model succeeded because it was designed to address the specific needs and fears of each community. By giving communities control over their cultural affairs and ensuring their representation in national government, Belgium transformed potential conflict into peaceful coexistence. This is an important lesson for CBSE Class 10 students: effective power sharing must be tailored to local conditions.

Sri Lanka: A Case Study in Power Sharing Failure (NCERT Case Study)

In stark contrast to Belgium, Sri Lanka demonstrates what happens when power sharing mechanisms are absent or poorly designed. This case study is critical for CBSE Class 10 exams and often appears in short-answer and long-answer questions.

The Sri Lankan Demographic Composition

Sri Lanka has a complex population: the majority Sinhalese population (74%) practices Buddhism, while Tamil minorities (18%) are predominantly Hindu and Christian. Additionally, there are Muslim minorities (7%). This diversity should have necessitated power-sharing mechanisms similar to Belgium's, but instead, Sri Lanka adopted a majoritarian approach.

Sri Lanka's Flawed Approach

After independence, Sri Lanka adopted a democratic system but failed to incorporate adequate power-sharing mechanisms for minorities:

  • No Federalism: The country remained a unitary state with centralized power. This meant that Tamil-majority regions had little autonomy over local affairs.
  • Majoritarian Democracy: The Sinhalese majority's preferences dominated the political system, often ignoring or marginalizing Tamil interests.
  • Official Language Policy: Sinhala was declared the official language in 1956, sidelining Tamil and angering the Tamil population. This linguistic discrimination fueled resentment.
  • Discriminatory Policies: Educational and employment policies often favored Sinhalese citizens over Tamils, creating systemic inequality.
  • Absence of Minority Protections: Unlike Belgium, Sri Lanka lacked constitutional provisions protecting minority rights and ensuring their representation in government.

Consequences of Failed Power Sharing

The absence of effective power sharing led to catastrophic consequences:

  • Communal Violence: Periodic riots and violence between Sinhalese and Tamil communities erupted, most notably in 1983 when anti-Tamil riots killed over 1,000 people.
  • Armed Conflict: Tamil frustration eventually led to the formation of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE), resulting in a devastating civil war that lasted 26 years (1983-2009).
  • Human Rights Abuses: The conflict resulted in massive casualties, displacement of populations, and widespread atrocities on both sides.
  • Stalled Development: The civil war devastated Sri Lanka's economy and infrastructure, hindering national progress.

Key Lessons from Sri Lanka's Experience

This case study teaches CBSE Class 10 students that democracies cannot afford to ignore minority rights. Power sharing is not an optional luxury but a necessity for peaceful coexistence in diverse societies. When majorities use their numerical advantage to marginalize minorities, the result is social breakdown and violence.

📌 Key Exam Tip: Common Mistakes to Avoid

Many students confuse Belgium and Sri Lanka or fail to explain why one succeeded and the other failed. Remember: Belgium succeeded because of constitutional federalism, community governments, coalition cabinets, and minority protections. Sri Lanka failed due to centralized unitary governance, majoritarian rule, language discrimination, and lack of minority safeguards. Always connect the mechanisms to the outcomes in your answers.
